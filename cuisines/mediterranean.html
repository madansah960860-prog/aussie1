Cuisine

Mediterranean

The style that fits Australia best. Much of the country shares a climate with southern Europe, grows the same produce at the same intensity, and has had Greek, Italian, Lebanese and Turkish cooking woven into its food for three generations.

Why it works here

Adelaide, Perth and much of Victoria have a Mediterranean climate in the technical sense: hot dry summers, mild wet winters. That is why Australian olive oil, tomatoes, stone fruit, eggplant and capsicum are as good as they are, and why the cooking that evolved around those ingredients translates without effort.

It is also cooking built around four things that are cheap: olive oil, acid, herbs and time. A tray of vegetables roasted hard and dressed while warm costs very little and tastes like a lot of care went into it. A ragù that takes three hours is mostly the oven doing the work.

Post-war migration did the rest. Greek and Italian communities in Melbourne, Sydney and Adelaide built the grocers, the delis and the small goods industry that make this food ordinary rather than exotic. Buying a good tin of tomatoes or a block of parmesan in Australia is easy because of them.

The pantry

Extra virgin olive oil, two bottles
A cheaper Australian bottle for cooking and a better one for finishing. Australian oil is often fresher than imported, because it has not spent months on a boat.
Tinned tomatoes
Whole peeled are usually better fruit than crushed. Crush them by hand into the pot.
Red wine vinegar
The default acid for dressings that will sit rather than be eaten immediately.
Dried oregano
One of the few herbs genuinely better dried than fresh. Buy it on the branch if you see it.
Capers and olives
Salt-packed capers rinsed well beat brined ones. Buy olives with the pits in.
Dried pasta and pulses
A bronze-die pasta holds sauce far better than a smooth one. Chickpeas and lentils, dried or tinned.
Tahini
Hulled tahini for dressings. Store it upside down so the oil redistributes.
Parmesan and feta
Buy parmesan in a wedge and keep the rinds in the freezer for braises. Note that traditional parmesan uses animal rennet.

Buying olive oil in Australia

Australian extra virgin olive oil is often the better buy, and not for patriotic reasons. Olive oil degrades with time, light and heat. Local oil is typically pressed within the last year and has not crossed the equator in a shipping container, so it is usually fresher than an imported bottle at the same price.

Three things to look for on the label. A harvest date, not just a best before, and ideally within the last twelve months. A dark glass bottle or a tin, since light destroys oil. And the words extra virgin, which is a legal grade with a chemical standard behind it, unlike terms such as pure or light, which describe refined oil.

Keep it in a cupboard, not next to the cooktop. The heat from a stove will age a good bottle in weeks. Buy the size you will use in two months rather than the biggest tin, unless you decant it.

Salt, acid and the fix for flat food

If a Mediterranean dish tastes dull, it is usually missing acid rather than salt. Add a squeeze of lemon or a teaspoon of vinegar and taste again before reaching for more salt. This single habit improves more home cooking than any piece of equipment.
