Asian at Home

Asia is not a cuisine. What this collection actually is: a handful of dishes we cook on weeknights, drawn mostly from Chinese, Vietnamese and Thai home cooking, written honestly as an Australian home cook's version rather than as the real thing.

A note on what this page is and is not

Roughly one in six Australians has Asian ancestry, and in most cities the best cooking of these cuisines is being done in homes and restaurants by people who grew up with it. We are not going to pretend otherwise, and we are not going to publish a recipe titled as authentic when it is a Melbourne kitchen's approximation.

So the recipes here are labelled for what they are. Our chicken, ginger and rice noodle soup is not pho. Pho is a specific dish with a long slow bone broth, charred aromatics and a set of garnishes, and it deserves the two hours it takes. Ours is a forty minute weeknight broth built on bought stock, and calling it what it is seems better than borrowing a name it has not earned.

If you want the real versions, buy from the people who cook them properly, and cook from writers who grew up in those kitchens. There is a lot of very good Australian food writing by cooks from Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Malaysian, Korean and Japanese backgrounds, and it is worth your money.

The short pantry list

Twelve things that will get you through most weeknight cooking in this style. All are available at Coles, Woolworths and Aldi, and all are cheaper and better at an Asian grocer if you have one nearby.

Light soy sauce
The everyday one. Salty and thin. Not the same as dark soy, which is thicker, sweeter and mostly used for colour.
Fish sauce
Buy a brand with two or three ingredients on the label. It smells alarming and tastes savoury rather than fishy in a finished dish.
Rice wine vinegar
Milder than white vinegar. Used for brightness at the end rather than for pickling.
Sesame oil
Toasted, dark, used by the teaspoon as a finishing oil. Not a cooking oil, it burns.
Shaoxing wine
Chinese cooking wine. Dry sherry is the usual substitute. Sold in the international aisle.
Oyster sauce
Thick and savoury. Vegetarian mushroom versions are widely available and very good.
Chilli oil or chilli crisp
A jar of it improves eggs, noodles, dumplings and roast vegetables.
Fresh ginger
Buy a large knob and keep it in the freezer, where it grates without stringing.
Garlic and spring onions
Bought weekly, used daily.
Rice noodles
Dried flat noodles for soups, thin vermicelli for salads.
Jasmine rice
Buy the biggest bag you have room for, it is dramatically cheaper by the kilo.
Star anise and white pepper
Two spices that do most of the work in broths.

Three techniques worth practising

Velveting

Tossing sliced meat with a teaspoon of cornflour, a teaspoon of Shaoxing wine and a little soy before it hits the pan. It takes two minutes and it is why restaurant stir-fried beef is tender and yours is not. The starch forms a thin coating that holds moisture through high heat.

Getting the pan hot enough

Home cooktops do not produce anything like the heat of a commercial wok burner. The practical fix is to cook in smaller batches than feels necessary and to accept that a heavy frying pan often beats a wok on an electric or induction hob, because it holds heat better. Do not add cold meat to a pan that has not recovered.

Balancing at the end

Salty, sour, sweet and hot are adjusted in the last thirty seconds, not at the start. Keep fish sauce, lime or vinegar, sugar and chilli within reach and taste before serving. Nearly every dish in this style that tastes flat is missing acid, not salt.

Where to shop

Every Australian capital has substantial Asian grocery precincts, and most large towns have at least one store. You will pay roughly half the supermarket price for soy, noodles, rice and fresh herbs, and the turnover means the produce is fresher. Bring cash for the smaller shops.
