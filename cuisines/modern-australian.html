Modern Australian

A label that has been used to mean almost anything. Here it means the way food is actually cooked and eaten in Australian homes: produce led, borrowed from everywhere, usually shared, and very often outside.

What we mean by it

Modern Australian is not a fixed set of dishes the way a regional Italian cuisine is. It is a way of cooking shaped by three things: an enormous range of produce available year round somewhere in the country, waves of migration that put fish sauce and sumac in the same pantry, and a climate that pushes people to eat outdoors for a good part of the year.

In practice that means a table with several dishes on it rather than a plated main. A barbecued protein, two or three vegetable dishes, something acidic, bread. Nobody is doing a first course. The salads are as considered as the meat.

It also means being honest about borrowing. A tray bake glazed with soy, ginger and honey is not a Chinese dish, and calling it one would be silly. It is a home cook in Melbourne using ingredients from the Asian grocer two streets away, in a technique learned from a European oven. That is genuinely what Modern Australian is, and it is worth naming rather than dressing up.

Native ingredients, used with some care

Lemon myrtle, wattleseed, saltbush, finger lime, Davidson plum, pepperberry and macadamia are all now easy to buy in Australian cities. They are worth cooking with, and they are also the food of the world's oldest continuing cultures. A few things worth keeping in mind.

  • Buy from Indigenous owned suppliers where you can. Several Aboriginal enterprises now sell native produce directly, and the money going back to those communities matters.
  • Use them because they taste good in the dish, not as decoration on top of something that did not need them.
  • Learn the actual name and where the plant grows. Lemon myrtle is not a substitute for lemongrass, it is its own thing, and it is much stronger than people expect.

Macadamia is the easiest starting point, because it is a familiar ingredient with a long commercial history, and it is genuinely Australian. It appears in our chocolate and macadamia cookies.

The share table, and how much food to cook

The most common mistake with a share table is cooking four main dishes. If everything is a centrepiece, nothing is, and half of it goes in the bin. A better shape for eight people is one substantial protein, two vegetable dishes where one is warm and one is raw, something starchy, and one sharp thing such as pickles or a lemony dressing.

Quantities that work for eight adults: about 1.2 kg of meat or fish off the bone, or 1.8 kg on the bone. Around 1.5 kg of vegetables in total across the dishes. A 500 g loaf of bread. That is generous without being ridiculous, and it leaves room for the fact that Australians tend to arrive with something.

Timing matters more than the menu. Anything that can be served at room temperature should be made first and left alone. That usually means the salads, the dressings and the bread. Keep the barbecue for the one thing that has to be hot.
