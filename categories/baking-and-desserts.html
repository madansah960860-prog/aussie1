Baking & Desserts

Weighed in grams, with a fan-forced temperature printed beside every conventional one. Baking is the part of cooking where guessing costs you the most, so this is the collection where we are strictest about measurement.

Why we weigh, and why the cups toggle still exists

A cup of plain flour, scooped straight from the packet, weighs about 175 g. Spooned in gently and levelled, the same cup holds about 150 g. That 25 g gap is roughly a sixth of the flour in a cake, and it is the reason two people can follow the same recipe and get very different results.

So every recipe here is written in grams first. The Metric and Cups toggle at the top of the page is there because plenty of Australian kitchens do not own scales, and a cup measurement you actually use beats a gram measurement you skip. When you use cups, spoon the flour in and level it off with a knife. Never scoop and never tap the cup on the bench.

Remember that Australian measures are their own thing. The Australian cup is 250 ml, larger than the American 240 ml cup. The Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, which is four teaspoons, while British, American and New Zealand tablespoons are 15 ml, or three teaspoons. In a batch of biscuits, using a 15 ml tablespoon where the recipe expects 20 ml will noticeably change the result. Our kitchen basics and conversions page has the full tables.

On ovens: fan-forced is normally set 20°C lower than conventional, and most domestic ovens in Australia run 10 to 20°C away from the number on the dial anyway. An oven thermometer costs about $15 AUD and is the cheapest improvement you can make to your baking.

Sprinkle Birthday Cake with Vanilla Buttercream

A reliable two layer butter cake that does not dome badly, with a buttercream that stays smooth. Made the day before, it is a much calmer birthday.

For the buttercream and finish

Method

  1. Prepare the tins

    Heat the oven to 180°C, or 160°C fan-forced. Grease two 20 cm round tins and line the bases with baking paper. Do not line the sides, the batter needs to grip them to climb.

  2. Cream properly

    Beat the butter and caster sugar for a full 5 minutes until very pale and fluffy. This is where the cake gets its lift, and five minutes is much longer than it feels.

  3. Add eggs one at a time

    Beat in the eggs one by one, waiting until each disappears before adding the next. Add the vanilla with the last egg. Room temperature eggs will not split the mixture.

  4. Alternate flour and milk

    Fold in the flour and salt in three additions, alternating with the milk in two, starting and finishing with flour. Fold gently by hand. Beating at this stage makes the cake tough.

  5. Bake and test

    Divide between the tins, level the tops, and bake for 33 to 38 minutes. A skewer in the centre should come out with a crumb or two, not wet batter. Do not open the oven before 30 minutes.

  6. Cool completely

    Cool in the tins for 10 minutes, then turn onto a rack and cool completely. Buttercream on a warm cake will slide off, and no amount of refrigeration fixes it afterwards.

  7. Make the buttercream

    Beat the very soft butter for 5 minutes until almost white. Add the sifted icing sugar in three lots, then the milk and vanilla, and beat for another 3 minutes. Sifting twice is the difference between smooth and gritty.

  8. Assemble

    Level the tops with a serrated knife if they have domed. Sandwich with a third of the buttercream, cover the top and sides with the rest, and press sprinkles onto the sides with the palm of your hand over a tray.

Cook's tips

  • Pure icing sugar, not icing mixture. Icing mixture contains cornflour, which makes buttercream taste chalky. Australian supermarkets sell both, side by side, and the labels are easy to confuse.
  • If the buttercream splits, warm it. Hold the bowl over a warm damp cloth for thirty seconds and keep beating. Split buttercream is almost always a temperature problem, not a ruined batch.
  • Bake both tins on the same shelf. Stacked shelves cook unevenly, and two cakes of different heights will not sandwich neatly.
  • Sprinkles bleed. Nonpareils and jimmies hold their colour. Soft sugar strands bleed into buttercream within a few hours, so add those at the last minute.

Storage and make ahead

Unfrosted layers keep, wrapped well, for 2 days at room temperature or 2 months in the freezer. Buttercream keeps 5 days in the fridge and needs a full hour on the bench plus a re-beat before use. The finished cake keeps 3 days in a cool spot, covered. Refrigeration dries butter cake out, so only refrigerate on a hot day.

Allergens. Contains gluten, dairy and egg. Sprinkles frequently contain soy lecithin and some contain shellac. Read the packaging in your own kitchen.

Passionfruit Curd Cupcakes

A plain vanilla cupcake is a delivery system for icing. Cutting a cone out of the middle and filling it with sharp passionfruit curd gives the sweetness something to argue with.

Method

  1. Make the curd first

    Whisk the passionfruit pulp, sugar and yolks in a heatproof bowl over a pan of barely simmering water. Stir constantly for 8 to 10 minutes until it thickly coats the back of a spoon, then whisk in the butter piece by piece. Cool and refrigerate.

  2. Line the tin

    Heat the oven to 180°C, or 160°C fan-forced, and line a 12 hole muffin tin with paper cases.

  3. Make the batter

    Cream the butter and sugar for 4 minutes, beat in the eggs one at a time with the vanilla, then fold in the flour and milk alternately until just combined.

  4. Bake

    Fill the cases two thirds full and bake for 18 to 20 minutes, until springy in the centre. Cool completely on a rack.

  5. Core and fill

    Cut a cone about 2 cm wide and 2 cm deep out of each cupcake with a small knife. Spoon in a teaspoon of cold curd. Trim the cone flat and put the lid back on.

  6. Ice and finish

    Beat the butter, icing sugar and milk for 5 minutes until pale. Pipe or spread onto each cupcake and top with a spoonful of the remaining curd just before serving.

Cook's tips

  • Do not let the curd bowl touch the water. Direct heat scrambles the yolks in seconds. If it does catch, push it through a sieve immediately and it is usually salvageable.
  • Use fresh passionfruit in season. Australian passionfruit is best from about October to April. Out of season, tinned passionfruit pulp works, but drain off some syrup or the curd will be too sweet.
  • Fill only cold cupcakes. Warm cake absorbs the curd and turns gluey, and warm cupcakes melt the icing.

Storage and make ahead

Curd keeps 1 week refrigerated in a clean jar and freezes for 2 months. Unfilled cupcakes keep 2 days in an airtight container. Iced cupcakes are best on the day and acceptable on day two. Do not freeze once filled.

Allergens. Contains gluten, dairy and egg. Read the packaging in your own kitchen.

Ricotta Hotcakes with Strawberries

Whipped egg whites folded through at the very end are the only reason these are light. Skip that step and you have a pikelet with ricotta in it.

Cook

4 serves

Moderate

Medium-low

Ingredients

Serves
4

Australian 250 ml cups. Spoon flour into the cup and level it, do not scoop.

For the hotcakes

To serve

0 of 10 ticked

Method

  1. Macerate the strawberries

    Toss the halved strawberries with the caster sugar and set aside. In 20 minutes they will make their own syrup.

  2. Mix the base

    Whisk the ricotta, milk and egg yolks in a large bowl. Sift in the flour, baking powder and salt and fold until just combined. It will look thick, which is correct.

  3. Whip the whites

    Beat the egg whites in a clean dry bowl to soft peaks. The peak should flop over when you lift the beater. Stiff peaks are harder to fold in without knocking the air out.

  4. Fold in three additions

    Stir a third of the whites through to loosen the batter, then fold in the rest in two lots with a light hand. Stop while you can still see a few streaks.

  5. Cook low and slow

    Melt a little butter in a frying pan over medium-low heat. Cook 8 cm rounds for 3 minutes, until bubbles form and the edges set, then flip and cook 2 minutes more. High heat burns the outside before the middle sets.

  6. Serve immediately

    Stack the hotcakes with the strawberries and their syrup and a spoonful of softly whipped cream.

Cook's tips

  • Use deli ricotta, not the tub. Tub ricotta is wetter and often has added gums, which makes the batter loose. If tub ricotta is all you have, drain it in a sieve for 30 minutes.
  • Wipe the pan between batches. Butter burns and the second batch picks up the colour. A quick wipe with paper towel and fresh butter keeps every hotcake looking the same.
  • Keep them warm at 100°C. Sit finished hotcakes on a rack in a low oven rather than stacking them on a plate, where the steam makes them soggy.

Storage and make ahead

Best eaten immediately. Cooked hotcakes keep 1 day and reheat reasonably in a 160°C oven for 5 minutes. The batter cannot be made ahead, because the whipped whites collapse within about 20 minutes.

Allergens. Contains gluten, dairy and egg. Read the packaging in your own kitchen.

Strawberry and Vanilla Yoghurt Pots

No oven, no gelatine, no piping bag. Six glasses assembled in twenty minutes and left in the fridge until you need them, which makes this the dessert we make most often in February.

Method

  1. Toast the crunch

    Toss the oats, almonds and honey in a frying pan over medium heat for 6 minutes, stirring often, until golden. Spread on a tray to cool and crisp. Do not walk away, honey catches quickly.

  2. Macerate the fruit

    Toss the chopped strawberries with the sugar and lemon juice and leave for 15 minutes until syrupy. Keep the sliced ones aside for the top.

  3. Whip the yoghurt cream

    Whip the cream to soft peaks, then fold in the yoghurt, icing sugar and vanilla. Fold rather than beat, or the yoghurt goes runny.

  4. Layer

    Spoon a little syrupy fruit into the base of six glasses, add yoghurt cream, then more fruit, finishing with cream. Refrigerate for at least 2 hours.

  5. Finish at the table

    Top with the sliced strawberries and a spoonful of the oat crunch just before serving so it stays crisp.

Cook's tips

  • Keep the crunch separate until serving. Toasted oats go soft in about fifteen minutes on top of yoghurt. Store it in a jar and scatter at the last second.
  • Full fat Greek yoghurt only. Low fat yoghurt has more water and the layers will weep into each other overnight.
  • Use whatever fruit is in season. Mango in January, stone fruit in February, poached pears in June. The method does not change.

Storage and make ahead

Assembled pots keep 2 days covered in the fridge. The oat crunch keeps 2 weeks in an airtight jar and is worth making a double batch of. Do not freeze, the yoghurt splits.

Allergens. Contains dairy and tree nuts (almond). Oats may carry gluten unless labelled gluten free. Read the packaging in your own kitchen.

Salted Caramel Chocolate Pots

A set chocolate cream with a spoonful of salted caramel underneath. Five ingredients, one saucepan, and a caramel that is far more forgiving than its reputation suggests.

6 serves

Serves
6

Australian 250 ml cups.

For the salted caramel

For the chocolate cream

0 of 8 ticked

Method

  1. Make the caramel

    Put the sugar and water in a clean saucepan over medium heat. Swirl the pan rather than stirring. Let it bubble for 6 to 8 minutes until it turns the colour of strong tea. Do not walk away in the last two minutes.

  2. Add cream carefully

    Take the pan off the heat and pour in the warmed cream slowly. It will hiss and rise dramatically. Stand back, then stir until smooth, add the butter and salt flakes.

  3. Divide the caramel

    Spoon a tablespoon of warm caramel into the base of six small glasses. Refrigerate while you make the chocolate layer. Keep the rest of the caramel for serving.

  4. Make the chocolate cream

    Heat the cream and milk until just steaming, not boiling. Pour over the chopped chocolate, wait 2 minutes without touching it, then stir from the centre outwards until glossy and smooth.

  5. Fill and chill

    Pour the chocolate cream gently over the set caramel. Refrigerate for at least 3 hours, or overnight.

  6. Serve

    Take the pots out of the fridge 15 minutes before serving. Drizzle with the remaining caramel and add an extra pinch of salt flakes.

Cook's tips

  • Warm the cream before it hits the caramel. Cold cream on hot sugar seizes the caramel into a solid lump. Thirty seconds in the microwave is enough.
  • Do not stir the sugar while it caramelises. Stirring encourages crystals. Swirl the pan by the handle instead, and if crystals form on the side, brush them down with a wet pastry brush.
  • Chop the chocolate finely. Large chunks do not melt fully in residual heat and you end up stirring endlessly, which cools the mixture and dulls the shine.

Storage and make ahead

Made a day ahead is ideal. The pots keep 3 days covered in the fridge. Leftover caramel keeps 2 weeks refrigerated and reheats gently in a saucepan or in short bursts in the microwave. Do not freeze the pots, the texture goes grainy.

Allergens. Contains dairy and usually soy (soy lecithin in chocolate). Read the packaging in your own kitchen.

No-Knead Overnight Bread

Five minutes of work the night before and a very hot casserole dish in the morning. The steam trapped under the lid is what gives you a crust you cannot get from a bare tray.

Prep
10 min
Rest
12 hr
Bake
45 min
Makes
1 loaf
Difficulty
Easy
Oven
240°C / 220°C fan

Ingredients

Loaves
1

Metric measures. Bread is the recipe where scales matter most.

Australian 250 ml cups. Weigh the flour if you possibly can.

For the dough

0 of 5 ticked

Method

  1. Mix the night before

    Stir the flour, water, yeast and salt in a large bowl with a spoon until no dry flour remains. It will be a shaggy, sticky mess. That is correct. Cover the bowl and leave it on the bench for 12 to 18 hours.

  2. Shape in the morning

    Tip the risen dough onto a well floured bench. Fold it over on itself four times to make a rough round, then sit it seam side down on a piece of baking paper dusted with semolina.

  3. Second rise

    Cover loosely with a tea towel and rest for 45 minutes while the oven heats. The dough should look puffy and spread slightly.

  4. Heat the pot properly

    Put a heavy casserole dish with its lid into the oven and heat to 240°C, or 220°C fan-forced, for at least 30 minutes. The pot must be genuinely hot before the dough goes in.

  5. Bake covered, then uncovered

    Lower the dough into the hot pot using the baking paper as a sling. Cover and bake for 30 minutes, then remove the lid and bake for another 15 minutes until deep brown.

  6. Cool before cutting

    Turn the loaf onto a rack and wait at least 45 minutes. Cutting hot bread squashes the crumb and lets the steam escape, which dries the loaf out.

Cook's tips

  • Adjust for the weather. In a Queensland summer, 12 hours on the bench is plenty. In a Melbourne winter, 18 hours is closer. The dough is ready when the surface is bubbled and it smells faintly sour.
  • Any lidded ovenproof pot works. Cast iron is best, but a heavy stainless pot with a metal lid or an ovenproof glass casserole will do. Check the lid knob is rated to 240°C, plastic ones are not.
  • Do not add more flour when shaping. The dough is meant to be wet. Flour your hands and the bench instead, and work quickly with a dough scraper.

Storage and make ahead

Keeps cut side down on a board for 2 days. Do not refrigerate bread, it goes stale faster. Slice and freeze for up to 3 months and toast from frozen. The overnight dough can be refrigerated for a further 24 hours after its first rise, which deepens the flavour.

Allergens. Contains gluten. Dairy free and vegan as written. Read the packaging in your own kitchen.

Banana and Blueberry French Toast

Day-old bread, a proper soak, and a pan that is cooler than you think it should be. Rushed french toast is raw in the middle and burnt outside, and there is no way to fix it once it is on the plate.

Prep
10 min
Cook
15 min
Total
25 min
Serves
4 serves
Difficulty
Easy
Heat
Medium-low

Ingredients

Serves
4

Metric measures. One Australian cup is 250 ml, one tablespoon is 20 ml.

Australian 250 ml cups.

For the custard

To cook and serve

0 of 10 ticked

Method

  1. Whisk the custard

    Whisk the eggs, milk, sugar, vanilla and cinnamon in a wide shallow dish until completely combined, with no streaks of white left.

  2. Soak, do not dip

    Soak each slice for 30 seconds a side. Day-old bread can take it. Fresh bread will fall apart, which is why this is a recipe for the end of the loaf.

  3. Cook gently

    Melt a little butter in a frying pan over medium-low heat and cook the slices for 3 minutes each side until deep golden. Higher heat browns the outside before the custard sets inside.

  4. Keep warm

    Hold cooked slices on a rack in a 100°C oven while you finish the rest. Wipe the pan and add fresh butter between batches.

  5. Serve

    Stack with sliced banana and blueberries and pour over the maple syrup.

Cook's tips

  • Stale bread is the point. If your bread is fresh, dry the slices in a 150°C oven for 8 minutes and cool them before soaking.
  • Strain the custard. Pushing it through a sieve removes the stringy bits of egg white, and the finished toast has a much smoother texture.
  • Real maple syrup, not maple flavoured syrup. Australian supermarkets sell both and the difference in taste is enormous. Check the ingredients list says maple syrup and nothing else.

Storage and make ahead

The custard can be mixed the night before and kept covered in the fridge. Cooked french toast keeps 1 day and reheats in a 170°C oven for 6 minutes, though it is never as good. Do not soak the bread ahead of time.

Allergens. Contains gluten, dairy and egg. Read the packaging in your own kitchen.
