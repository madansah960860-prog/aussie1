Fan-forced, conventional or grill: reading your oven properly

By Marlee Ashcroft. Published 04/08/2026. About an 11 minute read.

Two people cook the same tray of roast vegetables from the same recipe at the same temperature. One gets deep brown edges in thirty minutes. The other opens the oven at forty minutes to find pale, slightly damp vegetables and quietly assumes they did something wrong.

They probably did not. Ovens vary enormously, the symbol on the dial changes the outcome more than most people realise, and almost no domestic oven runs at the temperature it claims.

What the symbols actually mean

Australian ovens generally offer some combination of these settings, and the icons are more informative than they look.

Conventional, sometimes called static or bake

Two lines, one at the top and one at the bottom of a square. Heat comes from elements above and below with no air movement. The heat rises, so the top of the oven is hotter than the bottom, and food browns on the surface facing the nearest element. This is the setting most older recipes assume.

Good for: cakes, custards, anything you want to rise gently and evenly without a skin forming too early. Slow braises with the lid on.

Fan-forced, sometimes called fan bake or convection

A fan symbol, usually with a circle around it. A fan at the back of the oven circulates hot air, which strips away the cool layer that forms around food and transfers heat much faster. Temperature is far more even throughout the cavity.

Good for: roasting vegetables and meat, biscuits, anything where you want browning and crisping, and any time you are cooking on more than one shelf at once.

Fan grill

A fan symbol with a line above it. The top element and the fan run together. It browns hard and fast while moving air around, so it does not just scorch the surface directly under the element.

Good for: finishing a gratin, crisping chicken skin, blistering the top of a bake in the last few minutes.

Grill

A line at the top of a square, sometimes a zigzag. Radiant heat from above only, at full power. Nothing circulates. The distance from the element is the only control you have.

Good for: melting cheese, charring capsicum skins, toasting. Bad for anything that needs to cook through, because the outside is finished long before the inside has warmed up.

Bottom heat

A single line at the base. Common on European ovens and increasingly on Australian ones. Useful for setting a pastry base without colouring the top, and for pizza if you do not have a stone.

Why fan-forced is set 20°C lower

Because moving air transfers heat much more efficiently than still air. The same principle makes 10°C feel colder on a windy day than a calm one, and it is why a fan-forced oven at 180°C cooks roughly like a conventional oven at 200°C.

The standard adjustment, and the one used by nearly every Australian cookbook and food publication, is to set a fan-forced oven 20°C below the conventional temperature. Every recipe on this site gives both numbers so you never have to do the arithmetic. Our kitchen basics and conversions page has the full chart alongside gas marks.

Two useful caveats. The 20°C rule is a convention rather than a law of physics, and some manufacturers recommend 15°C or 25°C for their own ovens. And it applies to the temperature, not the time. Fan-forced usually also runs about ten to fifteen per cent faster at the adjusted temperature, so check earlier than the recipe says the first time you make something.

Your oven is probably not at the temperature on the dial

This is the part that surprises people. Domestic ovens are commonly 10 to 20°C away from their set temperature, and the error is often not consistent across the range. An oven can be accurate at 180°C and 25°C cool at 220°C.

The thermostat also cycles. It heats past the set point, switches off, drifts down, and switches back on. What the dial describes is an average, and the swing on a cheap oven can be 20°C in either direction. This is why a tray of biscuits can burn on the bottom in an oven that is, on average, correct.

The fix costs about $15 AUD. An oven thermometer, the kind that hangs from a shelf, tells you what is actually happening in there.

  1. Hang it in the middle of the middle shelf

    Not against the wall, not on the floor of the oven.

  2. Set the oven to 180°C and wait twenty minutes

    Most ovens beep when the element first reaches temperature, which is well before the cavity and the shelves have caught up. Twenty minutes is the honest preheat.

  3. Read it, then read it again ten minutes later

    You are looking for both the offset and the size of the swing.

  4. Write the offset on a sticky note inside a cupboard door

    If your oven runs 15°C cool, set it 15°C higher and stop thinking about it.

Hot spots, and what to do about them

Nearly every oven has them. The back is usually hotter than the front because that is where the element and the fan sit, and the top is usually hotter than the bottom in conventional mode.

There is a simple test. Cover a tray with slices of white bread, put it on the middle shelf at 200°C, and pull it out after about five minutes. The pattern of browning is a map of your oven. Almost everyone finds one corner noticeably darker.

The remedy is to rotate. Turn trays front to back halfway through anything that needs even colour, and swap shelf positions if you are cooking two trays at once. If your oven is very uneven, cook biscuits one tray at a time. Two trays at once in a domestic oven usually gives you one good tray and one disappointing one.

Preheating, and why the beep lies

The indicator light or beep on most ovens responds to a sensor near the element, not to the mass of air, metal and glass in the cavity. When it beeps, the air might be at temperature but the shelves, the walls and the door are not, and those store and radiate a large part of the heat that cooks your food.

For anything that depends on immediate high heat, give the oven a genuine twenty minutes past the beep. That includes bread, pizza, pastry, and roast vegetables you want crisp rather than steamed. If you are preheating a heavy tray or a cast iron casserole, make it thirty.

For a slow braise or a cake, the beep is close enough.

Six things that change the result more than the number on the dial

  • Crowding the tray. Food that is touching releases steam that has nowhere to go, and steamed food does not brown. Use two trays.
  • Baking paper under things you want crisp. Paper insulates the base slightly and stops direct contact with hot metal. Excellent for biscuits. Wrong for roast potatoes.
  • A cold tray. Putting food onto a tray that has been preheating gives an immediate sear on the underside. This is most of the difference between good and great roast potatoes.
  • Opening the door. Each opening drops the cavity temperature substantially, and it takes minutes to recover. Use the light.
  • Shelf position. Middle for most things, lower third for anything with a base that needs to set, upper third for browning.
  • Wet food. Surface moisture must evaporate before browning starts. Pat meat dry, dry your chickpeas, and salt eggplant in advance.

When to ignore the recipe temperature

A recipe temperature is the writer's best guess for a generic oven, which is not the one in your kitchen. Override it when the evidence in front of you says to.

If the top is colouring far too fast while the middle is raw, drop the temperature by 20°C and move the shelf down a level. Cover with foil if it is already dark.

If nothing is browning at all and the time is nearly up, your oven is running cool. Raise it by 20°C for the last ten minutes, or switch to fan grill for the final three, watching it constantly.

If the recipe is American, note that it will be written in Fahrenheit for a conventional oven. 350°F is 180°C conventional, which is 160°C fan-forced. Converting the Fahrenheit number but forgetting the fan adjustment is a common way to overcook something by a wide margin.

Cooking times in any recipe, including ours, are a guide. The descriptions matter more: golden brown at the edges, a skewer that comes out with a crumb or two, 75°C at the thickest part. Those are true in every oven. The numbers on the dial are only true in one.
