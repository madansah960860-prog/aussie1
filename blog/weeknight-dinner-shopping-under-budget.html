Five dinners, one shop: how we plan a week without wasting half the coriander

By Marlee Ashcroft. Published 11/08/2026. About a 14 minute read.

The bin is where most household food money goes. Not the expensive cut of meat, not the good olive oil, but the half bunch of coriander, the tired half of a red cabbage, the yoghurt bought for one recipe and never opened again. Australian households throw out a meaningful share of the food they buy, and almost all of it was purchased with good intentions.

The fix is not a spreadsheet or an app. It is a way of choosing five dinners so that the leftovers of one become the beginning of another. We have been planning this way for years and it takes about fifteen minutes on a Saturday.

Start with the constraint, not the recipes

Most meal planning fails because it starts with a wish list. Five recipes that all look appealing, chosen independently, will need five separate sets of fresh herbs, three different vinegars and two kinds of cheese. You will use a quarter of each.

So start with the actual constraints of the week instead.

  • Which nights are genuinely short? Not which nights you wish were short. If Tuesday is sport and Thursday is late meetings, those two dinners must be under thirty minutes or they will become takeaway.
  • Which single night has an hour or more? That is where the long cook goes, and it should be one that produces leftovers.
  • What is already in the fridge and the freezer? Look before you plan, not after you shop.
  • Who is actually eating? Four dinners for four people plus one for two is a different shop to five for four.

Only now do you pick recipes, and you pick them to fit the shape you have just described.

The overlap rule

This is the whole method. Every perishable ingredient you buy should appear in at least two dinners, or in one dinner and one lunch.

Herbs are the obvious case. A bunch of coriander is enough for three dishes and rots in five days. So if Monday is a noodle soup that needs coriander, Wednesday should be something that also wants it, such as a tray bake finished with fresh herbs. If nothing else in the week wants coriander, either change one dinner or accept that you are buying it for one meal and plan to use the stems in a stock.

The same applies to anything sold in a quantity larger than one recipe uses.

Anchor the week on one long cook

Pick one dish that takes hours but almost no attention, cook it on the day you have time, and make more than you need. A slow-cooked beef ragù that serves six takes the same twenty minutes of active work whether you make four serves or eight. Two portions in the freezer is a Friday night you do not have to think about.

This is where cheap cuts earn their reputation. Chuck steak, gravy beef, lamb shoulder, chicken thigh and pork shoulder are all substantially cheaper per kilogram than the quick-cooking cuts, and they are all better after two or three hours. The trade is time you are not spending in the kitchen anyway, because the oven is doing it.

The second thing a long cook gives you is a base. A pot of braised meat becomes pasta on Sunday, a jaffle filling on Tuesday and a baked potato topping on Thursday without anyone feeling like they are eating leftovers three times.

Shop the way the shop is laid out

Write the list grouped by section, not by recipe. Fruit and vegetables, butcher, fridge, freezer, pantry. A list organised by recipe sends you back and forth across the shop, which is how the extra items end up in the trolley.

Then check the pantry before you leave. The single most common waste in a planned week is buying a second jar of something you already have three quarters of. Keep a running list on the fridge of things that are actually running out.

A few notes on where to buy, since this varies more in Australia than people assume.

  • The greengrocer is usually cheaper than the supermarkets for anything genuinely in season, and the produce moves faster so it lasts longer at home. Ask what is good today.
  • Aldi is generally the cheapest for staples, dairy, tinned goods and frozen fruit and vegetables. The range is narrower, which for a planned shop is not a problem.
  • Coles and Woolworths are convenient and consistent, and their half price specials are worth planning around if the item is a staple you would buy anyway.
  • The butcher will sell you the cheap cuts the supermarket does not stock, cut them how you want, and tell you what is good this week. This is where the biggest savings on protein are.
  • Asian grocers are dramatically cheaper for rice, noodles, soy sauce, fresh herbs and vegetables, often half the supermarket price.

Buy seasonally, and let the price decide

The clearest signal that something is in season is that it is cheap and there is a lot of it. If capsicums are $12 a kilogram in August, that is the market telling you to cook something else. Our seasonal produce guide lists what is at its best month by month, written for Southern Hemisphere seasons where summer runs from December to February.

This is also where the quality argument lines up with the money argument. A February tomato and a July tomato are not the same product. Cooking to the season is not a virtue exercise, it is how you get better food for less.

Frozen deserves a mention here too. Berries, peas, corn and spinach are snap frozen within hours of harvest. Out of season they beat tired fresh produce on both price and taste, and they do not rot in the crisper while you decide what to do with them.

The four habits that cut the most waste

  1. Store herbs like flowers

    Stems in a glass of water, a bag over the top, in the fridge. Coriander and parsley will hold for a week and a half instead of four days. Basil is the exception and stays on the bench.

  2. Keep a freezer shelf for halves

    Half a tin of tomatoes, the last of the stock, the parmesan rind, coriander stems, ginger, chilli. All of it freezes and all of it goes back into a braise or a soup later.

  3. Cook the fragile things first

    Order the week so that the softest produce is used earliest. Leafy salads and berries on Monday and Tuesday. Root vegetables, cabbage and pumpkin later in the week, because they will still be fine on Friday.

  4. Leave one night unplanned

    A five night plan across a seven night week leaves room for leftovers, for the night nobody is hungry, and for the night you were going to be organised and were not. Planning all seven guarantees waste.

Putting it together

A worked example from a recent week. Sunday had time, so the ragù went in the oven and the whole pumpkin was roasted at the same time on the shelf below, which cost nothing extra in gas or electricity. Monday was a chicken and ginger noodle soup, which used half the ginger and half the coriander. Tuesday was pumpkin soup, already ninety per cent done because the pumpkin was roasted. Wednesday was a chicken tray bake using the rest of the ginger, the rest of the coriander and the second half of the soy sauce bought for Monday. Thursday was salmon with a caper dressing, twenty five minutes, using the parsley bought on Sunday.

Five dinners, one shop, nothing bought twice, and the only thing that went in the bin was the pumpkin skin. The full list for that week, grouped by aisle, is on our meal plans page.

None of this requires you to eat the same five things forever. The method is the constant, not the menu.
