Why your biscuits spread: Australian cups, tablespoons and the 20 ml problem

By Marlee Ashcroft. Published 18/08/2026. About a 12 minute read.

A reader wrote to us last month about a batch of Anzac biscuits that had spread into one continuous sheet across the tray. They had followed the recipe carefully. They had used the right oven temperature. They had chilled the dough. The recipe was from a well known American site, and the problem was one line: two tablespoons of golden syrup.

In Australia, two tablespoons is 40 ml. In the United States, where the recipe was written, two tablespoons is about 30 ml. That is an extra third of a very sticky, very fluid sugar syrup in a dough that was already at the edge of what it could hold together. The biscuits did exactly what physics said they should.

This is the most common measurement problem in Australian kitchens, and almost nobody is told about it until it costs them a batch of something.

The numbers

An Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, which is four teaspoons. The United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and New Zealand all use a 15 ml tablespoon, which is three teaspoons. Australia is the outlier, and has been since metrication in the 1970s, when the tablespoon was standardised at 20 ml rather than the 15 ml most other metric countries landed on.

The cup is a smaller problem. An Australian cup is 250 ml. An American cup is 240 ml, a difference of four per cent. A British imperial cup, if you meet one in an old book, is 284 ml, which is a much bigger gap, but British recipes have overwhelmingly used weight for decades so you will rarely run into it.

The teaspoon is the one measure everyone agrees on. Five millilitres, everywhere, near enough.

So the practical rule is simple. Cups are close enough for most things. Tablespoons are not, and the error compounds every time the word appears in an ingredients list.

Where 5 ml per spoon actually matters

Not everywhere. If a stir-fry calls for two tablespoons of soy sauce and you use 40 ml instead of 30 ml, you have a slightly saltier stir-fry and you will not notice at the table. Nobody has ruined a braise this way.

The places it bites are the ones where an ingredient is doing chemical work rather than adding flavour.

Raising agents

Baking powder and bicarb soda are the worst offenders because they are used in small quantities with a large effect. A recipe calling for one tablespoon of baking powder means 15 ml if it is American. Measured with an Australian tablespoon, you have added 20 ml, a third more than intended. The cake rises fast, the structure cannot hold it, and it collapses in the middle as it cools. It also tastes faintly metallic and soapy, which is the bicarb that had nothing to react with.

Salt

A third more salt in a bread dough will slow the yeast noticeably. A third more salt in a brine will cure rather than season. In a dough or a batter, where you cannot taste and adjust at the end, this is not recoverable.

Yeast

Excess yeast does not make bread rise better. It makes it rise faster, develop less flavour, and often collapse. Yeast quantities in bread recipes are deliberate.

Thickeners and syrups

Cornflour, gelatine, golden syrup, treacle and honey all change texture rather than flavour. The Anzac biscuit disaster above is exactly this. So is the panna cotta that will not set and the sauce that turns to glue.

Strong spices and extracts

A third more ground cloves, cardamom or vanilla extract is very noticeable. These are ingredients where the recipe writer chose a specific amount because more is genuinely worse.

How to convert an overseas recipe safely

The method we use is to convert everything to teaspoons and millilitres before starting, and write the converted numbers on the page or in a note on the phone. It takes two minutes and removes the problem entirely.

  1. Work out where the recipe is from. Spelling gives it away quickly. Cilantro, all purpose flour, baking soda, broiler and zucchini in an otherwise American voice means a 15 ml tablespoon. Australian sites will say coriander, plain flour, bicarb soda, grill.
  2. Convert every tablespoon to teaspoons. One American or British tablespoon is three teaspoons. Two is six teaspoons. Three is nine.
  3. Measure in teaspoons. The teaspoon is identical in both systems, so nine teaspoons is nine teaspoons wherever the recipe came from.
  4. For liquids, use a jug. A 60 ml quarter cup measure or a small measuring jug is more accurate than counting spoons for anything over about 30 ml.
  5. For flour and sugar, ignore the volume and find a weight. Most reputable sites now publish grams alongside cups. If they do, use the grams and stop thinking about it.

Going the other way, from an Australian recipe to an American kitchen, the same logic applies in reverse. Our tablespoon is four teaspoons, so three Australian tablespoons is twelve teaspoons, which is four American tablespoons.

The bigger problem: a cup of flour is not a fixed amount

Even within one country, volume measurement of flour is unreliable, and this causes more failed baking than the tablespoon issue ever will.

Try it yourself. Take a 250 ml cup measure and dip it into the flour bag, scooping and pressing down slightly, then level it off. Weigh it. Now spoon flour loosely into the same cup, level it with the back of a knife, and weigh that.

The scooped cup will typically weigh 170 to 180 g. The spooned cup will weigh around 150 g. That is a difference of nearly twenty per cent, from the same flour, in the same cup, measured by the same person two minutes apart.

In a cake with three cups of flour, that is 90 g of extra flour, which is close to two thirds of an extra cup. It is the difference between a tender crumb and something dry and tight that everyone politely finishes.

This is why every baking recipe on this site is written in grams first, and why the cups toggle on our recipe pages assumes flour is spooned and levelled rather than scooped. It is also why a $20 AUD set of digital scales does more for your baking than any other purchase at any price.

The one exception worth knowing

Liquids are genuinely fine by volume. Water, milk, oil, stock and juice all measure accurately in a jug, because liquids cannot be compressed or aerated the way flour can. If a recipe is entirely liquid measures and whole eggs, cups are perfectly good and scales add nothing.

It is dry ingredients where volume falls apart, and specifically the light, compressible ones: flour, icing sugar, cocoa, desiccated coconut, oats and grated cheese. Granulated sugar is surprisingly consistent, because the crystals do not compact much, which is why sugar in cups causes far fewer problems than flour in cups.

What we do

Every recipe on Sunlit Pantry is written in Australian metric measures, weighed where weight matters. The Metric and Cups toggle in the utility bar on each recipe page converts the quantities to the Australian 250 ml cup and 20 ml tablespoon, using the ingredient weights listed on our kitchen basics and conversions page. Oven temperatures are given as conventional with the fan-forced equivalent beside them.

None of this is about being fussy. It is about the fact that a recipe is a set of instructions from someone who is not in the room with you, and the more precisely the amounts are described, the better your odds of getting what they got.

The reader with the flat Anzac biscuits made them again with the syrup measured in teaspoons. They reported back that the second batch was fine.
