Australian Seasonal Produce Guide

Written for the Southern Hemisphere. Summer here runs from December to February, autumn from March to May, winter from June to August and spring from September to November. Most recipe sites get this exactly backwards.

How to use this guide

Australia spans tropical, subtropical, arid and temperate zones, so nothing is in season everywhere at once. Mangoes start in the Northern Territory in September and reach southern markets in November. Tomatoes crop in Queensland through winter and in Victoria through summer. This guide reflects when produce is at its best and cheapest in the major southern markets, which is where most of the population shops.

The most reliable signal is price. When something is genuinely in season, it is cheap, and there is a lot of it. If capsicums are $12 a kilogram in August, that is the greengrocer telling you to cook something else. Buying in season is the single easiest way to eat well for less money, and it produces better food, because a tomato that ripened on the plant in February tastes like a different vegetable to one picked green in July.

Frozen is not a compromise for most fruit. Berries, peas, corn and spinach are snap frozen within hours of picking, so out of season they usually beat tired fresh produce on both taste and price.

The four seasons, Southern Hemisphere

Summer, December to February

Fruit. Mango, stone fruit (peach, nectarine, apricot, plum), cherries, watermelon, rockmelon, berries, passionfruit, lychee, grapes, figs late in the season.

Vegetables. Tomatoes at their peak, corn, zucchini, capsicum, eggplant, cucumber, beans, basil, lettuce.

Seafood. Prawns, particularly around Christmas. Whole fish for the barbecue.

Cook. Tomato bruschetta, barbecued corn, summer fruit platter.

Autumn, March to May

Fruit. Apples, pears, figs, late grapes, quince, persimmon, early mandarins, feijoa.

Vegetables. Pumpkin, mushrooms, silverbeet, broccoli, cauliflower, leek, fennel, late eggplant and capsicum.

Seafood. Mussels, squid, snapper.

Cook. Roast pumpkin soup, pumpkin and chickpea salad.

Winter, June to August

Fruit. Citrus at its best: navel oranges, mandarins, blood oranges, lemons, grapefruit. Kiwifruit, rhubarb, late apples and pears.

Vegetables. Brassicas of every kind, root vegetables, leek, silverbeet, kale, brussels sprouts, potatoes, pumpkin.

Seafood. Oysters, blue swimmer crab in the north, salmon.

Cook. Slow-cooked beef ragù, chicken and ginger noodle soup.

Spring, September to November

Fruit. Strawberries, early stone fruit, mangoes arriving from the north, pineapple, papaya, late citrus.

Vegetables. Asparagus, peas, broad beans, new season garlic, spring onions, artichokes, early lettuce, broccolini.

Seafood. Whiting, flathead, school prawns.

Cook. Green goddess salad with broccolini, ricotta hotcakes with strawberries.

Growing seasons shift with the weather in any given year, and vary considerably between Cairns, Perth and Hobart. Treat this as a starting point and let the price at the shops be the final word.

Buying well, wherever you shop

The greengrocer
Usually cheaper than the supermarkets for anything in season, and the turnover is faster so the produce is fresher. Ask what is good today. They will tell you honestly, because they want you back next week.
Coles, Woolworths and Aldi
Consistent and convenient. Aldi is often the cheapest for staples and for frozen fruit and vegetables. Check the country of origin labelling, which is required on most fresh produce in Australia.
Farmers markets
Best for seasonal peaks and for varieties the supermarkets do not carry. Not always cheaper. Go late for the end of day prices, or early for the pick of it.
The butcher and fishmonger
Better value on cheaper cuts, and they will do the work for you. Ask what is good rather than arriving with a fixed list.

Storing produce so it lasts

  • Tomatoes never go in the fridge. Below about 12°C they lose flavour and turn mealy. Bench, stem side down.
  • Herbs keep like flowers: stems in a glass of water, a bag over the top, in the fridge. Basil is the exception and stays on the bench.
  • Potatoes and onions in a dark cupboard, but not together. Onions make potatoes sprout.
  • Berries unwashed until you eat them, in a single layer if you can.
  • Leafy greens washed, spun dry, and rolled in a clean tea towel in the crisper. They will hold for a week.
  • Bananas away from everything else. They give off ethylene and will ripen the whole bowl.
