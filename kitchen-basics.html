Kitchen Basics & Conversions

Australian measures are not the same as American or British ones. This page has the numbers you need, why the differences matter, and the oven and ingredient tables we use ourselves.

The two numbers that catch people out

One Australian cup is 250 ml. The American cup is 240 ml and the British cup, where it is used at all, is 284 ml. In most savoury cooking a 10 ml difference is invisible. In baking, across three or four cups of liquid, it is not.

One Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, which is four teaspoons. This is the important one. The United States, the United Kingdom and New Zealand all use a 15 ml tablespoon of three teaspoons. Australia is the outlier.

That means an American recipe calling for three tablespoons of baking powder, oil or salt will give you a third more than intended if you use an Australian tablespoon. In a cake with two teaspoons of raising agent, using Australian tablespoons where the recipe meant American ones adds a full extra teaspoon, and the cake will rise fast and collapse.

The teaspoon is the one measure everyone agrees on: 5 ml, everywhere.

The simple rule

If a recipe is from an overseas site and uses tablespoons for anything powerful, such as baking powder, bicarb soda, salt, yeast or strong spices, convert it to teaspoons first. Three American tablespoons is nine teaspoons. Then measure nine teaspoons, not three Australian tablespoons.

Oven temperatures

Fan-forced ovens circulate hot air, so they cook faster and more evenly than conventional ones. The standard adjustment is to set a fan-forced oven 20°C lower than a conventional recipe temperature. Every recipe on this site gives both.

Two practical notes. Most domestic ovens are 10 to 20°C away from the temperature on the dial, and the error is rarely consistent across the range. An oven thermometer costs about $15 AUD and tells you what your oven actually does. Second, ovens are hotter at the back and near the top, so rotate trays halfway through anything that needs even colour.

Equipment worth owning

Short list, in the order we would buy it. None of this is essential to cook well, and a great deal of very good food has been made without any of it.

  1. Digital scales

    Around $20 AUD. Fixes more baking problems than any other purchase. Look for one that measures in 1 g increments and has a tare button.

  2. An instant read thermometer

    Around $30 to $40 AUD. Takes the guesswork out of chicken, pork and fish, and stops you overcooking expensive cuts.

  3. One heavy roasting tray

    Thin trays buckle at high heat and pool sauce in one corner. One good heavy tray outlasts five cheap ones.

  4. A sharp knife and a steel

    A blunt knife is more dangerous than a sharp one because it slips. Learn to hone weekly and get it properly sharpened twice a year.

Food safety basics

General guidance only. For current official advice see the Food Standards Australia New Zealand and your state health department websites.

  • Keep the fridge at or below 5°C and the freezer at or below minus 15°C.
  • Cool cooked food quickly and refrigerate within two hours. In hot weather, one hour.
  • Thaw frozen food in the fridge, not on the bench.
  • Reheat leftovers until steaming hot all the way through, and only reheat once.
  • Keep raw meat below and separate from ready to eat food in the fridge.
  • Wash hands, boards and knives between raw meat and anything eaten raw.

This is general information, not medical or food safety advice for a specific situation. See our nutrition and allergen disclaimer.
